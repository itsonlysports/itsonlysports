Every week, millions of people open DraftKings and ask the same question: who's going to score the most points?

It's the wrong question. Not because it doesn't matter, but because everybody is asking it. And when everybody asks the same question, they all land on the same answer.

## The math nobody wants to do

Here's the whole idea in one line: what a player is worth in a tournament is **the chance he goes off, times how much of the field you pass when he does.**

That second half is the part people skip. If a guy is on 40% of rosters and he hangs 35 points, congratulations. You moved up alongside 40% of the field. You didn't beat them. You tied them.

Now flip it. Say there are two receivers on the slate:

- **Receiver A** has roughly an 18% chance of a ceiling game and is on 35% of rosters.
- **Receiver B** has roughly a 12% chance of a ceiling game and is on 5% of rosters.

Receiver A is the better player. Nobody's arguing that. But divide the ceiling chance by the ownership and Receiver B wins easily, about 2.4 to 0.5. When B hits, you're passing 95% of the field. When A hits, you're stuck in a crowd.

(Those numbers are made up to show the math, not a projection for any real player.)

That ratio, ceiling probability over ownership, is what I call leverage. It's the number I care about most.

## "So just fade the chalk?"

No. That's the other mistake, and it's just as lazy.

Chalk isn't wrong. Most of the time chalk is chalk *because it's a good play*. The cheap running back who stepped into a starting role because of an injury is popular for a reason. If you fade every popular player, all you're doing is picking worse players on purpose.

The point is to be different **somewhere that matters**. Eat the chalk that deserves it. Then find the one or two spots where the field is lazy, where the ownership is way out of line with the actual odds, and put your chips there.

## Size of the room

How different do you need to be? That depends entirely on how many people you're up against.

- **A 12-person office contest:** one smart pivot is plenty. Don't overthink it.
- **A 150,000-entry tournament:** you need combinations almost nobody else built. Winning lineups at that size routinely carry two or more players under 10% owned.

Same strategy, different dose. The bigger the room, the weirder you have to get.

## Where this doesn't apply

I'll say it plainly: if you're setting your lineup in a 10-team season-long league, ignore most of this. Nobody's "ownership" matters in your head-to-head matchup. You want the guy with the best median outcome, the safest bet to give you points.

The one exception is when you're the underdog that week. If your opponent's roster is projected to beat yours by 20, safe and steady just loses by a little. That's the week to take the boom-or-bust receiver. Variance is your friend when you're behind.

## The takeaway

Next time you build a lineup, don't stop at "who's good?" Ask the follow-up: **"Who's good that nobody else has?"**

That's the whole game.
